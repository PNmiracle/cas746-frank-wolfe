1 · Motivation

SGD and weight decay

Blue: SGD steps downhill. Red (click 3): weight decay also pulls \(\theta\) toward 0.
SGD is still the default, with the weights \(\theta\) left unconstrained.
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\class{tA}{\theta_t}\class{tB}{-\alpha\, g_t} \]
Adaptive methods (Adam, Adagrad) train faster early on, but often generalize worse (Wilson et al., 2017).
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\class{tD}{(1-\lambda)}\,\class{tA}{\theta_t}\class{tB}{-\alpha\, g_t} \]
Remember the factor \((1-\lambda)\): it comes back in Frank–Wolfe.
1 · Motivation

Idea: constrain the weights instead

If penalizing big \(\theta\) helps, why not require \(\theta\) to stay in a compact convex set \(C\)?
\[ \min_{\theta\in \class{tC}{C}}\; L(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_i(\theta) \]
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\class{tC}{\Pi_C}\big(\class{tB}{\theta_t-\alpha\, g_t}\big) \]
\[ \class{tC}{\Pi_C}(x)=\arg\min_{v\in C}\ \class{tE}{\|v-x\|_2} \]
Frank–Wolfe avoids projections altogether.

Gray: the SGD step lands outside C. Red: projection back to the closest point.

Dashed: the smallest circle around that point that touches C.

2 · Stochastic Frank–Wolfe

Frank–Wolfe: a projection-free step

\[ \class{tE}{v_t}=\arg\min_{v\in \class{tC}{C}}\ \langle \class{tB}{g_t},\, v\rangle \]
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\class{tA}{\theta_t}+\class{tD}{\alpha}\,(\class{tE}{v_t}-\class{tA}{\theta_t}),\quad \alpha\in[0,1] \]
\[ \class{tA}{\theta_t}\in C,\ \class{tE}{v_t}\in C\ \Rightarrow\ \theta_{t+1}\in C \]
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\class{tD}{(1-\alpha)}\,\class{tA}{\theta_t}+\class{tE}{\alpha\, v_t} \]

Why projection-free? LMO vs. projection

L2 ball \(\{x:\|x\|_2\le\tau\}\)

What it means · \(g/\|g\|_2\) is the gradient’s direction scaled to length 1; the minus sign turns it downhill; \(\tau\) stretches it to the edge of the ball. So the LMO returns the point of the ball furthest downhill.

Why · By Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\langle g,v\rangle\ge-\|g\|_2\|v\|_2\ge-\tau\|g\|_2\), with equality exactly at \(v=-\tau g/\|g\|_2\).

Projection \(\tau x/\|x\|_2\): a point outside is just shrunk back onto the sphere. Both are one line.

Hypercube \(\{x:|x_i|\le\tau\ \forall i\}\)

What it means · \(\mathrm{sign}(g)\) replaces every entry of \(g\) by \(+1\) or \(-1\). So each weight goes to \(-\tau\) if its gradient is positive, \(+\tau\) if negative: a corner of the cube.

Why · \(\langle g,v\rangle=\sum_i g_i v_i\) and each \(v_i\) is chosen separately in \([-\tau,\tau]\); each term is smallest at \(v_i=-\tau\,\mathrm{sign}(g_i)\).

Example: \(g=(0.8,-0.3)\Rightarrow v=(-\tau,+\tau)\). Projection: clip each entry to \([-\tau,\tau]\). Both trivial.

L1 ball \(\{x:\sum_i|x_i|\le\tau\}\)

What it means · Find the single entry with the largest \(|g_i|\) and put the whole budget \(\tau\) there, with the opposite sign: \(v=-\tau\,\mathrm{sign}(g_{i^*})\,e_{i^*}\). All other entries are 0.

Why · The budget \(\sum|v_i|\le\tau\) is best spent where it lowers \(\langle g,v\rangle\) the most: on the steepest coordinate.

Example: \(g=(0.8,-0.3)\Rightarrow v=(-\tau,0)\). Projection is harder: soft-thresholding with a threshold found by sorting (Duchi et al., 2008).

\(L_p\) ball \(\{x:\|x\|_p\le\tau\}\)

What it means · Every entry moves against its gradient, with size proportional to \(|g_i|^{q-1}\): large gradients get large weights. The denominator \(\|g\|_q^{q-1}\) rescales \(v\) onto the boundary. Here \(q\) is the dual exponent, \(1/p+1/q=1\).

Why · The equality case of Hölder’s inequality \(\langle g,v\rangle\ge-\|g\|_q\|v\|_p\).

It interpolates: \(p=2\Rightarrow q=2\), the L2 rule; \(p\to\infty\Rightarrow q\to1\), the cube rule \(-\tau\,\mathrm{sign}(g)\). The projection has no closed form.

K-sparse polytope \(B_1(\tau K)\cap B_\infty(\tau)\)

What it means · Entries at most \(\tau\) in size, with total at most \(\tau K\). The LMO takes the \(K\) largest \(|g_i|\), sets those entries to \(-\tau\,\mathrm{sign}(g_i)\), and the rest to 0.

Why · Like the L1 rule, but up to \(K\) coordinates can each take \(\tau\): \(K=1\) is the L1 ball, \(K=n\) the hypercube.

Cost: one partial sort of \(|g|\). The paper gives no closed-form projection.

Permutahedron \(\mathrm{conv}\{\text{permutations of }(1,\dots,n)\}\)

What it means · Its corners are all orderings of \(1,\dots,n\). The LMO picks the ordering that makes \(\langle g,v\rangle\) smallest: the largest numbers go where \(g\) is most negative.

Why · This is an assignment problem; the paper solves it with the Hungarian method (polynomial time).

Projection needs specialized algorithms (Yasutake et al., 2011; Lim & Wright, 2016).

Takeaway: the LMO is usually a cheap, coordinate-wise rule, about as costly as an SGD step. Projections often need an algorithm of their own.

2 · Stochastic Frank–Wolfe

Algorithm 1: Stochastic Frank–Wolfe (SFW)

Input: \(\theta_0\in C\), step sizes \(\alpha_t\), momentum \(\rho_t\), batch sizes \(b_t\), steps \(T\)
1\(m_0\leftarrow 0\)
2for \(t=0\) to \(T-1\) do
3sample \(i_1,\dots,i_{b_t}\) uniformly from the training set
4\(g_t\leftarrow \frac{1}{b_t}\sum_{j=1}^{b_t}\nabla \ell_{i_j}(\theta_t)\)
5\(m_t\leftarrow (1-\rho_t)\,m_{t-1}+\rho_t\, g_t\)
6\(v_t\leftarrow \arg\min_{v\in C}\langle m_t, v\rangle\) LMO (4)
7\(\theta_{t+1}\leftarrow\theta_t+\alpha_t(v_t-\theta_t)\) step (5)
8end for
\(g_t\) is unbiased: \(\mathbb E[g_t]=\nabla L(\theta_t)\).
A drop-in replacement for an SGD optimizer (PyTorch and TensorFlow code released). With momentum, it is called MSFW.

2 · Stochastic Frank–Wolfe

Convergence: the Frank–Wolfe gap

Without constraints we stop when \(\nabla L(\theta)=0\). With constraints the optimum can sit on the boundary of \(C\) with \(\nabla L\neq 0\), so we need another measure.
\[ L(v)\;\approx\;\class{tA}{L(\theta)}+\langle \class{tB}{\nabla L(\theta)},\,\class{tC}{v-\theta}\rangle \]
\[ \class{tA}{L(\theta)}-\big[\class{tA}{L(\theta)}+\langle\nabla L(\theta),v-\theta\rangle\big]=\langle \class{tC}{v-\theta},\,\class{tB}{-\nabla L(\theta)}\rangle \]
\[ G(\theta)=\class{tE}{\max_{v\in C}}\ \langle \class{tC}{v-\theta},\,\class{tB}{-\nabla L(\theta)}\rangle \]
\[ \class{tE}{v^*}=\arg\min_{v\in C}\langle\nabla L(\theta),v\rangle\ \Rightarrow\ G(\theta)=\langle \class{tB}{\nabla L(\theta)},\,\theta-\class{tE}{v^*}\rangle \]
\[ \class{tE}{G(\theta)}\ge 0,\qquad \class{tE}{G(\theta)}=0\iff \theta \text{ is stationary} \]
\[ \mathbb E\big[\class{tE}{G(\theta_a)}\big]=O\!\left(\frac{\class{tF}{L(\theta_0)-L(\theta^*)}}{\class{tD}{\sqrt T}}\right) \]
Caveat: the theorem needs batch size \(T\) and no momentum; the experiments use fixed batches and momentum. A guide, not a guarantee.
Goal: a stopping test that works on the boundary, where \(\nabla L\neq0\).
Equations used
paper §1(4) LMO
\(v_t=\arg\min_{v\in C}\langle g_t,v\rangle\)
paper §2(7) Frank–Wolfe gap
\(G(\theta)=\max_{v\in C}\langle v-\theta,-\nabla L(\theta)\rangle\)
Smooth = the gradient is Lipschitz. Gap ≥ 0: take v = θ.

3 · Practical choices

Practical choice 1: a tunable step size

Dashed: \(v-\theta\). Red: plain step, same \(\alpha\). Green: rescaled step (14).
\[ \|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t\|=\class{tD}{\alpha}\,\class{tE}{\|v_t-\theta_t\|} \]
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\min\!\Big(\tfrac{\class{tD}{\alpha}}{\class{tG}{D(C)}},1\Big)(v_t-\theta_t) \]
\[ \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t+\min\!\Big(\tfrac{\class{tD}{\alpha}\,\class{tB}{\|g_t\|}}{\class{tE}{\|v_t-\theta_t\|}},1\Big)(v_t-\theta_t) \]
\[ \|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t\|=\frac{\class{tD}{\alpha}\class{tB}{\|g_t\|}}{\class{tE}{\|v_t-\theta_t\|}}\cdot\class{tE}{\|v_t-\theta_t\|}=\class{tD}{\alpha}\,\class{tB}{\|g_t\|} \]
Region size: tied to each layer's random initialization, times a width \(w\) (10 to 100). Momentum 0.9 (\(\rho=0.1\)); batch sizes as for SGD.

5 · Critique

Strengths and limitations (my reading)

Strengths

Simple. Drop-in replacement for SGD: only the LMO is new. Public code; runtime about the same as SGD.

A design tool. Choose sparse or dense updates, and shape what is learned. Tested at scale, including ImageNet.

Limitations
Theory ≠ practice.
Theorem 2.1Experiments
batch size\(T\) (grows)fixed
momentumnone (\(\rho=1\))0.9 (\(\rho=0.1\))

New hyperparameter. The width \(w\) is tuned per model (10 to 100): tuning moves, it doesn't disappear.

ImageNet. Weight decay still wins (74.89 vs. 73.46; 76.09 vs. 75.77), no error bars. One constraint type for every layer.

5 · Takeaways

Takeaways

1SFW trains deep networks with constrained weights: no projections, one cheap LMO per layer.
2With L2 constraints it beats plain SGD and roughly matches weight decay on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and ImageNet.
3The region shapes the network: K-sparse gives sparse weights, the hypercube dense ones.
4Simple SFW with momentum beats the variance-reduced variants per gradient.
5Recipe: step rescaled by (13) or (14), region size tied to the initialization, momentum 0.9.

Practical choice 2: how big should the region be?

Ties in the LMO
If several vertices solve the LMO (e.g. a zero gradient), pick one at random on the boundary. Then SFW can even train a zero-initialized layer in a hypercube.
Same constraint type for all layers; only the size varies, tied to the initialization (Glorot / He).
\[ \class{tB}{\mathbb E\|x\|_2}=\frac{n\,\sigma\,\Gamma\!\big(\frac n2+\frac12\big)}{\sqrt2\,\Gamma\!\big(\frac n2+1\big)} \]
\[ \class{tG}{D(C)}=2\class{tM}{w}\;\class{tB}{\mathbb E\|x\|_2} \]
The width \(w\) is a new hyperparameter: \(w=10\) to \(100\) in the deep-network runs.
